Sun outages

Sun-outage calculator

Twice a year the sun passes behind each geostationary satellite as seen from a given place, and the dishes pointed at it lose the signal for a few minutes a day. These losses are natural and predictable months ahead. A loss inside a window is expected; a loss outside one deserves a second look.

The whole arc: which latitudes are in outage, day by day

For a receiver looking at a satellite on its own meridian, the outage happens near local noon on the days when the sun's declination matches the satellite's apparent declination. That picks out one band of latitude per day, in each hemisphere. The band sweeps from the equator towards the poles and back each season, and the same band holds for every satellite on the arc, with times shifted by longitude. Satellites far east or west of a receiver shift its band slightly.

A short loss at the predicted time does not rule out interference at the same moment. It only means the loss itself is not evidence of interference.

Method and limits

  • Geometry only. Sun position from a low-precision solar ephemeris (Meeus, accurate to about 0.01°). Each satellite is treated as exactly geostationary at its nominal longitude; the receiver sits on the WGS84 ellipsoid at sea level. An outage is the sun's centre within the threshold angle of the satellite.
  • Threshold. Half the dish's 3 dB beamwidth (about 70 × wavelength ÷ diameter, in degrees) plus the sun's radius (0.27°). A 0.9 m dish at 12 GHz gives about 1.2°. Larger dishes get shorter, deeper fades; C-band dishes have wider beams and longer outages.
  • Edges can shift. Real satellites move within a ±0.1° station-keeping box, so window edges can shift by a day and peak times by a minute or two.
  • Checked. This page runs the same calculation as the project's Python tool (tools/sun_transit.py); an automatic test compares the two on six receiver and satellite pairs in both hemispheres. The Python tool was itself checked against a separately written calculator.
  • Strength varies. How deep a fade gets depends on how bright the sun is at that frequency, which changes with solar activity. The home page shows NOAA's live solar radio measurements beside the predicted windows.
