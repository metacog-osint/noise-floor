How to read this board

Read this first. Public elements place a satellite at GEO only to within tens of kilometres, so no separation under about 50 km is claimed, and a listed pair may be farther apart or closer than shown. Operators also place satellites close together on purpose, including across companies by agreement, and a servicing vehicle docked to its client shows as 0 km. Close approaches by inspection satellites are what this board is meant to show. A short list of satellites with a published inspection or servicing role is labelled, with sources; every other pair is shown as having no listed role.

Every active object in the geosynchronous belt, from public orbital elements: where it is, which are moving, which changed their orbit, and which sit close to a satellite of another owner. It shows proximity and behaviour. It never states intent.

Method

  • Data. CelesTrak's active geosynchronous group (GP elements in OMM format) and its catalogue records for owner and object type, taken once or twice a day by the daily run, within CelesTrak's usage policy. The elements are USSPACECOM basic SSA data, which Space-Track allows to be redistributed and used in published analysis with citation.
  • Longitude. The sub-satellite longitude from the mean elements, carried to a common time with two-body motion. Good to a few tenths of a degree, which is tens of kilometres at GEO.
  • Classes. Station-kept: drift under 0.1° a day and inclination under 1°. Inclined: drift under 0.1° a day, inclination 1° or more (often older satellites saving fuel). Drifting: 0.1° a day or more.
  • Close. Objects within 100 km of each other at the common time. CelesTrak's owner codes are often a country, so pairs with the same code are counted, not listed, unless one has a listed role. Elements older than 3 days are flagged beside the pair.
  • Manoeuvres. Between consecutive snapshots: the drift rate changed by more than 0.02° a day, the inclination by more than 0.02°, or the longitude is more than 0.15° from where the earlier elements put it at the later time (by the same orbit motion, so an inclined orbit's daily swing isn't counted). A change is routine station-keeping when the satellite isn't drifting before or after. Inclined satellites still trim their drift to hold their longitude, so that is routine too. A change to their inclination isn't, because they aren't held north–south. Everything else is listed first: a satellite that started or stopped drifting, or a drifting one whose drift rate changed. The dates are those of the two element sets; the change happened somewhere between them. Snapshots began on 26 Oct 2026.
  • Region. Everything Space-Track lists with a mean motion of 0.9 to 1.1 revolutions a day and an eccentricity under 0.3. The protected zone is the IADC geosynchronous protected region: perigee and apogee within 200 km of 35,786 km altitude, and inclination 15° or less. The graveyard is everything above it. Each day's list is compared with the day before: newly catalogued (launched within the last year), back in the public catalogue, entered the zone, raised to the graveyard, lowered below the zone, left the zone, dropped out of the list, and, for payloads in the zone, no new public elements for over 7 days. A satellite missing from public data hasn't necessarily stopped working: some are simply not published.
  • Owners are CelesTrak's codes: CIS is Russia and the former Soviet states, PRC China, ITSO Intelsat, EUTE Eutelsat, SES SES.

Orbital data: USSPACECOM (space-track.org) via CelesTrak, and from space-track.org directly for the region and its arrivals and departures; basic SSA data, redistributed with citation. Region code: tools/geo_region.py. Code: tools/geo.py. Data file: geo.json.
