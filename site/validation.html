A finding: downstream effects by aircraft type, and what's behind it

Over 17 days, A350-900s were 24.9% of aircraft-days still degraded after leaving interference, against 1.3% of traffic (19.4×). A380s were 19.2× and 777-300ERs 4.7×; most narrowbodies were near 1×.

Tested on 23 Oct 2026, this looks like a reporting effect. In 19 days of raw traces over the southern Baltic, A350s at cruise altitude stayed visible on 89% of their crossings on jammed days, while narrowbodies went dark on half of theirs; 74% of the positions the A350s kept sending were flagged as degraded. On quiet days every type was seen about equally, yet A350 degraded episodes lasted a median of 8 minutes against 2 for other types. A documented behaviour fits: Australia reported to an ICAO regional meeting in June 2025 that on A350s and A380s the ADS-B accuracy value is set to zero when the inertial reference unit flags a single satellite as degraded, even when the position is valid (CNS SG/29 IP/05). So the type pattern most likely shows how these aircraft report accuracy, not lasting harm. Next: count A350 and A380 accuracy-only drops apart and rerun the validated days.

Graded events (events v1.1, rules not yet validated)

What the methods above observe in one place and time window is joined into an event, and every event is graded on five fields: signal type, support, localization, attribution and verdict. The rules were frozen as version 1.0 on 26 Oct 2026; version 1.1 (27 Oct) added EASA's weekly FIR tables as a source for "deliberate suspected" and changed nothing else. They are stated choices, not yet tested against held-out cases. The register of events stays private; only these monthly counts are published.

  • Support. "Multiple sources" needs two independent sensor families agreeing in place and time (aircraft and CYGNSS). "Partially corroborated" is one sensor family plus an official notice consistent with it, such as an announced FAA GPS test or EASA's weekly listing of the FIR. Support says how well an event is evidenced, not what caused it.
  • Verdict. Planned: every effect lies inside an announced FAA test window and its FL250 radius, the one the map draws. Benign natural: a 10 m-only effect during a geomagnetic storm (poleward of a latitude gate, or inside a storm-flagged SBAS area) or with ICAO-moderate ionospheric scintillation in ESA's nowcast; worse-than-93 m loss is never excused this way. Deliberate suspected: an official notice reporting GNSS jamming or spoofing covers the event, or EASA's weekly table lists spoofing in the event's FIR that week (FIRs are approximated by country outlines). Spoofing is deliberate by nature, so the listing, not our data, is the evidence of intent, at the scale of a FIR and a week; EASA's table may itself draw on ADS-B data. EASA tables exist for 13 weeks of this period (its current table, and earlier copies kept by the Internet Archive), so this count follows which weeks have a table, not how much spoofing there was: months without one show zero. Open: none of these.
  • No grade names a perpetrator, and no event has a position fix yet.

Validation still to come

  • A localizer that resolves sites, not just areas. It failed its first scored test against the 2025 TDOA fixes, and its positions stay off the map until it passes one: two-emitter fits and fix-window timing were tried and dropped, and a heading-balanced third version improved on the second but didn't beat a constant guess. Next: days with a single known active site, scored at about 10 km.
  • More announced test events as known positives. The FAA advisories are already a layer on the map. Jammertest 2026 showed no aircraft effect.
  • More false look-alikes, beyond the Kiruna radar fault, as cases the methods must not flag.
  • A surveyed emitter or announced test to measure NISAR position accuracy.

Each case is checked against its source before it's scored.
